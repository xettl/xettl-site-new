Credit from zero · Practical guide

How to start building U.S. credit from zero

Three important parts of a U.S. credit file are whether payments arrive on time, how much available revolving credit is being used, and how long accounts have been open.

The most useful fact: You do not need to carry a balance or pay interest to build healthy credit. Paying on time and keeping reported card balances low matter more.

Reports first, scores second

Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion maintain credit reports. Scoring companies use information in those reports to calculate scores. You can have more than one score because lenders use different models and versions.

Can you begin without an SSN?

An SSN helps providers verify and match your identity, but it is not what creates credit history. Some providers may accept other documentation and may report an eligible account without an SSN. Ask what is accepted and which credit bureaus receive account data before applying.

Debit card or secured credit card: which comes first?

A debit card spends money already in your deposit account. It is useful for daily purchases and cash access, but it usually does not build credit. A secured credit card is a credit account backed by a refundable deposit; it can build history when the issuer reports to the nationwide bureaus.

A practical sequence: Establish a checking account and reliable bill-payment system first. Add a secured card only when you can monitor it, keep spending within your budget, and pay the statement balance on time. Compare annual fees, deposit rules, bureau reporting, upgrade paths, and refunds.

You do not need both on the same day. A debit card provides access to money in your account. An eligible secured card that reports to the nationwide credit bureaus can help establish credit history. Neither requires you to carry debt.

How different forms of credit affect you

Credit cards and revolving credit

Your balance changes as you spend and repay. The amount reported compared with your limits can affect scores. Paying the statement balance in full by the due date can avoid purchase interest on cards with a grace period.

Auto, personal, and other installment loans

Installment credit is repaid on a schedule. On-time payments can help and late payments can harm. Do not borrow solely to add another account type.

Authorized-user accounts

Some issuers report authorized-user activity. A well-managed older account may help, but a high balance or missed payment can also create risk.

Rent reporting

Rent is not always reported automatically. Optional services may report eligible payments. Check cost, bureaus covered, and whether missed payments are also reported.

A simple maintenance routine

  1. Pay every bill on time. If you use autopay, monitor the linked account so the payment does not fail. Paying the full statement balance by the due date can generally avoid purchase interest when a grace period applies.
  2. Keep reported card balances low compared with limits.
  3. Review reports for errors through AnnualCreditReport.com.
  4. Apply selectively and use prequalification when available.
  5. Freeze your reports if identity theft is a concern.
  6. Avoid companies promising to remove accurate information or create a new credit identity.

Primary sources: CFPB: understand your credit score, CFPB: get and keep a good score, and AnnualCreditReport.com.
