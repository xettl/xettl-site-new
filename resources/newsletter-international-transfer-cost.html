What that international transfer really costs you

Two providers can both advertise a $0 fee to send the same amount home, and your family can still receive two different amounts. The gap may be in the exchange rate, not the fee line.

An international transfer can include a provider fee, an exchange-rate markup, third-party deductions, and taxes. A service advertising “no fee” may still earn revenue through its exchange rate. Another service with a visible fee may deliver more.

For many remittance transfers sent from the United States, federal rules require disclosures before payment that include the exchange rate, applicable fees and taxes, and the amount expected to reach the recipient. Those numbers let you compare two quotes on equal terms.

Why “no fee” is not the same as “no cost”

A provider may earn revenue through the exchange rate even when it charges no separate transfer fee. A spread built into the exchange rate is a common way to price a transfer. It may not appear in a line item labeled “fee,” which is why the amount the recipient gets is the better comparison.

How to get a comparable quote

  • Get both quotes within a few minutes of each other. Rates move, and a quote from this morning is not comparable to one from this afternoon.
  • Use the same send amount, funding method, and delivery speed for both, since each one can change the quoted rate.
  • Compare the amount the recipient actually gets, not the fee or the rate in isolation.
This week's action: Get two live quotes for the same transfer, compare what the recipient actually receives, and check whether the better one is a one-time promotion or the provider's normal rate.

For a new route, send a small test first and save the receipt. If the disclosed amount or delivery date is wrong, contact the provider promptly.
